Replication of Rigobon & Sack (2003), *The Effects of War Risk on U.S. Financial
Markets*, applied to the **2026 Iran war** (window **28 Feb 2026 → present**).
The unobservable war-risk factor is recovered with
**identification-through-heteroskedasticity** (Rigobon 2003), using **NLP on war
news** (GDELT volume + FinBERT + a war lexicon) to flag high/low news days — the
paper's hand-read newspaper list, automated.

---

## 1. Task and approach

The professor's brief: *"Use NLP on war news to flag high-news days (1 = high,
0 = low), then apply heteroskedasticity-based identification. The war-risk
effect comes from the shift in variance on those days, so the direction of each
story doesn't need to be known."* Deliverables: replicate the paper's three
tables for the Iran war (28 Feb → present), and evaluate whether
heteroskedasticity-based identification is the best approach.

Two design choices follow from this:

1. **NLP only selects days.** I do *not* build a continuous war-risk index. A
   GDELT daily volume series (six war phrases) flags **H** (high-news) vs **L**
   (low-news) trading days; direction is used only for the three-regime
   extension, not for identification.
2. **Global variables.** The 2003 paper used nine U.S. variables; I use a global
   set (U.S. Treasuries + inflation breakeven, U.S. and European equities,
   Brent oil, gold, the dollar, and U.S. credit spreads), normalizing on the
   **2-year Treasury yield** exactly as the paper does.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 NLP flagging

For each trading day I compute a war-news intensity (the max article count across
the six war phrases, so a day is "high" if *any* war theme spikes). Days in the
top decile of intensity are **H** (flag = 1); all other trading days are **L**
(flag = 0). This is the professor's 1/0 flag. For the three-regime extension
(§5), headlines on H days are classified with a war lexicon plus **FinBERT**
(ProsusAI/finbert) sentiment into *bad* (escalation) / *good* (de-escalation).

### 3.2 Heteroskedasticity-based identification

For each pair (anchor Δx₁ = 2Y yield, variable Δx₂ = j), the reduced form is
Δx = D·z + μ with the war-risk factor z₁ normalized to a unit impact on Δx₁.
The covariance matrix is Ω = DΣzD′ + Ση. Under the identifying assumptions
(war risk orthogonal to other factors; only its variance shifts across H/L),

```
ΔΩ = Ω_H − Ω_L = Δσ²(z₁) · [[1, d],[d, d²]]
```

so the loading d of variable j on war risk is recovered two ways:

```
d_w1 = ΔΩ21 / ΔΩ11        (instrument built from the anchor)
d_w2 = ΔΩ22 / ΔΩ21        (instrument built from variable j)
d_w3 = inverse-variance-weighted combination
```

Variances are mean squares (mean of Δx², mean of Δx₁·Δx₂), matching the paper's
footnote 12. Standard errors are bootstrapped (resampling H and L separately,
3 000 draws), so the t-statistics are robust to the small H sample and to
unequal H/L sizes. Reported coefficients are multiplied by **−0.25**, i.e. the
effect of a war-risk *increase* large enough to move the 2Y yield **−25 bp**.

Table 3 reports the variance decomposition: `predicted change = d²·ΔVar(2Y)`,
`% on H days = pred/Var_H(xⱼ)`, and `% on all days = n_H·pred / (n_H·Var_H +
n_L·Var_L)` (a lower bound, assuming daily independence).

## 4. Results

All three tables are generated by `scripts/04_estimate.py` into `outputs/`.
Two normalizations are reported, because the choice of anchor turns out to be
the central empirical issue (§4.1).

### 4.1 The 2Y anchor fails for 2026 — a finding, not a bug

The paper normalizes the war-risk factor on the **2-year Treasury yield**, i.e.
it reads the factor's variance shift off the 2Y yield's variance shift.  That
requires the 2Y yield's variance to **rise** on high-news days.  In 2026 it does
not:

| Anchor | Var on H days | Var on L days | ΔΩ₁₁ | identification |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DGS2 (paper) | 0.002393 | 0.002647 | **−0.000254** | ✗ fails |
| BRENT | 63.13 | 12.77 | **+50.36** | ✓ holds |

The war-news days (early March) were a *flight-to-quality* period in which
Treasury yields were unusually **calm**; the yield volatility arrived later
(April–September: inflation, debt, the Warsh Fed), and those days are the L
sample.  The 2003 "war risk ⇒ Treasury volatility" channel is therefore absent
in 2026 — exactly the professor's prior.  Every *other* variable does have
higher variance on H days (oil, equities, gold, FX, credit spreads).

I report the paper-faithful **2Y-anchored** version as a diagnostic
(`outputs/table2_sensitivity.csv`) and the **Brent-anchored** version as the
substantive estimate (`..._BRENT.csv`); Brent is also the more natural anchor
for an Iran (energy-chokepoint) shock.

### 4.5 Verification (`scripts/05_verify.py`)

The whole chain was re-derived bottom-up from the raw files rather than trusted:

1. **Data integrity** — every change column is reproduced exactly by
   differencing its level column on the *anchor's* trading calendar.  (This
   check originally failed for the HY OAS because FRED's series carries a few
   weekend/holiday rows — e.g. Sunday 31 May — which were being differenced
   against the wrong neighbour.  Fixed by restricting each series to the
   anchor's calendar *before* differencing; the results then barely moved.)
2. **Rank-1 signature** — the single-factor model predicts
   `ΔΩ₂₁² = ΔΩ₁₁·ΔΩ₂₂`, i.e. the ratio `r` should be ≈ 1.  Measured:
   **r = 0.98** (HY OAS), **0.80** (Euro Stoxx), **0.79** (S&P 500), 1.16
   (gold), 0.67 (dollar), 0.68 (BBB).  The structure holds.  Yields give
   `r = nan` only because their `ΔΩ₂₂ < 0` (§4.1).
3. **Recomputation** — `d_w1`/`d_w2` recomputed by hand reproduce the saved
   table for all nine variables.
4. **Placebo** — the real H split's anchor-variance shift is **+50.36**; 5 000
   random 14-day splits give mean +0.03, sd 10.4, max +56.8
   (**p = 0.0002**).  The NLP flag therefore selects genuinely high-variance
   days, not arbitrary ones.
5. **Threshold sensitivity** — from `--quantile` 0.80 to 0.95 (n_H = 27 → 7)
   the coefficients barely move: Euro Stoxx −2.4 to −3.0, HY OAS +0.106 to
   +0.134, dollar +0.41 to +0.59.  The result is not an artefact of the cut.
6. **Variance-decomposition algebra** — `pred = d²·ΔVar(anchor)` and `% on H`
   reproduce Table 3 exactly.

Run it with `uv run python scripts/05_verify.py`.

### Three-regime extension — data-limited

Within H days, split by NLP direction (bad/good war news).  The classification
in `data/day_regimes.csv` returns **13 bad days and only 1 good day** (8 Apr
ceasefire), because every other high-news day falls inside the March
escalation.  The "good war news" regime therefore has n = 1 and **cannot be
estimated separately** — effectively only "bad war news vs no war news" is
identified.  This is a property of the episode (a hot-war window), not of the
method, and is reported as a limitation rather than a result.

## 6. Is heteroskedasticity-based identification the best approach?

*Asked and answered honestly (the professor's second deliverable).*

**Why it is appropriate here.**

1. **The factor is unobservable.** War risk cannot be quantified ("today's war
   risk = 7.3"), so any approach that needs a measured level is out. Het-ID needs
   only *which days* had elevated war-news variance — exactly what NLP provides.
2. **It handles simultaneity without an instrument.** Asset prices and the war
   narrative are jointly determined; het-ID uses the *second-moment* shift as a
   probabilistic instrument, so no exclusion restriction is required.
3. **It is robust to regime misspecification.** Rigobon (2003, Prop. 3–4) shows
   the estimator stays consistent if the H/L split is imperfect (as long as the
   covariance matrices still differ in rank) — important when the day-flag comes
   from a noisy NLP classifier.
4. **No proxy = no attenuation.** A text-based war-risk index would be measured
   with error, biasing OLS loadings toward zero.

**Why it is *not* obviously "best" for the 2026 exercise.**

1. **Long-window confounding.** The paper's window was 10 weeks of *pre-war
   buildup*. Mine is ~6.5 months spanning war onset, an active phase, and a
   simmer-down — over which *other* factors' variances (Fed policy under Warsh,
   inflation/debt shocks, oil-supply shifts) also changed. If H = "hot-war March"
   and L = "calm September", the assumption that *only* war-risk variance shifts
   is strained, and d can pick up the March-vs-September macro shift.  This is
   the central threat to validity in this exercise, and its empirical signature
   is visible in §4.1: the 2Y yield's variance *falls* on H days, so the paper's
   own anchor no longer identifies the factor.
2. **One factor, many things.** War news is multidimensional (likelihood,
   duration, energy disruption); collapsing it to one factor discards the
   direction and composition — the paper itself concedes this.
3. **Pairwise estimation.** I (like the paper) estimate two variables at a time
   for small-sample reasons, sacrificing the overidentifying restrictions a
   multivariate het-ID would give.
4. **Direction discarded.** The core method ignores whether news is good or bad,
   so it averages over both — the three-regime split is only a partial fix.

**Alternatives, evaluated.**

| Alternative | Idea | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct text index + OLS/IV | Regress returns on a daily war-risk surprise (e.g. Caldara–Iacoviello GPR, or a custom FinBERT war score) | Uses level *and* sign; simple; dynamic | Measurement error → attenuation; needs an "expected news" benchmark to get the *surprise* |
| Event study | Abnormal return around discrete war events | Standard, transparent, clean causality | Needs clean event dating; overlaps; no "per-unit-of-risk" loading |
| Structural VAR / sign restrictions | Identify a war shock via signs (oil↑, equities↓…) | Multivariate, dynamic IRFs | Only set-identified; signs must be assumed; not tied to actual news |
| High-frequency / intraday ID | Use minutes around a headline timestamp | Gold-standard causality (news = exogenous surprise) | Needs intraday data + precise timestamps; labor-intensive; not a daily factor loading |
| Multivariate het-ID (GMM) | N variables at once → overidentifying restrictions + parameter-stability test | Natural upgrade; testable | Needs more H days than we have |

**Verdict.** For *replicating the paper* (the assignment's explicit ask) with
NLP day-flagging, het-ID is the right and only directly-applicable method —
and it is the only one that needs no quantification of war risk. But I would
**not** call it unambiguously the best estimator of the *causal* 2026
sensitivities: the long-window confound means I would report it alongside (a) an
event study on the discrete escalation dates and (b) a FinBERT/GPR *surprise*
index regressed via IV, and treat intraday identification as the gold standard
if the data were available. The het-ID numbers are best read as "the shift in
second moments on war-news days", which is precisely what the professor asked
for, and no more than that.

## 7. Limitations

- **Regime confound** (§6): war onset vs. post-war macro drift is not separable
  in a single H/L contrast over 6.5 months.
- **Flag threshold is arbitrary** (top decile by default); the H set and thus d
  move with `--quantile`. A robustness table across quantiles belongs in the
  final notebook.
- **NLP noise — and a multilingual surprise.** GDELT volume counts *mentions*,
  not uniquely war-relevant stories, and FinBERT is finance-tuned rather than
  geopolitics-tuned.  More concretely: the first headline pull carried **no
  language filter** and came back **97% non-English** (210 headlines from 28
  countries; only 7 from U.S./UK sources), which the English lexicon and FinBERT
  could not score — 3% lexicon hits, 10/14 days "unclear", FinBERT `fb_neg` ≈
  0.03 (everything read as neutral).  Adding `sourcelang:english` plus a
  client-side language guard produced **350 English headlines, 21% lexicon hits,
  and FinBERT `fb_neg` of 0.10–0.68**; the direction classification only became
  usable after that fix.  This is the professor's "novel phrasing" warning in a
  broader form — a novel *vocabulary* and a *multilingual* corpus both degrade
  an English-centric pipeline.  It also reinforces why het-ID suits this task:
  the core result needs only *which* days were news-heavy, not what the stories
  said.
- **Three-regime split is data-limited.** 13 of the 14 H days are escalation
  news, so the "good war news" regime has n = 1 and cannot be estimated (§5).
- **Sample size.** ~14–30 H days yields wide bootstrap standard errors; point
  estimates are more reliable than t-stats here.
- **Unequal H/L sizes.** The moment-based estimators handle this correctly, but
  the bootstrapped standard errors assume the H and L samples are each
  representative of their regime.
